UNIT 01 · BUILD THE MODEL

From story → decision model

Before calculating anything, learn to translate a paragraph into the five objects that make every DAB problem solvable.

The five things you must extract

Decision alternatives are choices controlled by the decision maker. States of nature are outcomes the decision maker cannot control. Payoff/cost tells you what each decision earns or costs under each state. Probabilities may be absent, prior, or posterior. Criterion tells you how to choose when probabilities are available or unavailable.

On a pen-and-paper exam, write these five headings before touching the arithmetic. This prevents the most common error: calculating a perfect number for the wrong model.

The exam-script habit
Start with: Decision: …   States: …   Payoff: …   Probabilities: …   Criterion: …
Do not confuse
A “state of nature” is not another decision. Demand = a state. Order 1/2/3 lots = decisions.

UNIT 03 · PROBABILITY + VALUE

EMV, EPPI, EVPI and EOL

Once probabilities exist, expected value becomes the backbone. Learn the arithmetic, but more importantly learn the relationships so you can check your own answer.

EMV(di) = Σ p(sj) × payoffij
Expected value with perfect information
At each state, pretend you already know the state and select the best payoff in that column. Then weight those best payoffs by their probabilities.
EPPI = Σ p(s) × maxd[payoff(d,s)]
EVPI
EVPI = EPPI − maximum EMV without perfect information.
EVPI = EPPI − max EMV

Cost problems: do not flip the rule accidentally

For a cost table, the best cell in a state is the minimum cost, not the maximum. You can work entirely in costs: choose the decision with minimum expected cost and compute perfect-information expected cost from the state minima.

UNIT 04 · DECISION TREES

Rollback: solve from right to left

A decision tree is just a payoff problem made sequential. Chance branches carry probabilities; decision branches contain choices. Solve the rightmost branches first, then roll values backward.

The three-node language
Decision node (square): choose the best branch. Chance node (circle): take the probability-weighted average. Terminal node: actual payoff/outcome.

UNIT 05 · BAYES + SAMPLE INFORMATION

Update beliefs without getting lost

Bayes problems look frightening because they have two probability systems: the original belief and the posterior belief after information. Once you keep them separate, the calculation becomes mechanical.

PriorP(state)What you believed before the signal.
LikelihoodP(signal | state)How the signal behaves under each state.
PosteriorP(state | signal)What you believe after seeing the signal.
Total probabilityP(signal)=Σ P(signal|state)P(state)Needed when posterior is not directly supplied.

Why EVSI is not “the cost of research”

First calculate the best EMV without information. Then calculate the expected value after observing each possible signal and choosing the best action for that signal. The difference is EVSI. Only after that do you compare EVSI with the research cost.

UNIT 06 · NETWORK LOGIC

CPM from zero: forward pass

CPM is not “draw a network and guess the longest line.” You must compute earliest times and latest times systematically.

Forward pass

For a start activity, ES = 0. EF = ES + duration. If an activity has several predecessors, its ES is the maximum EF of all immediate predecessors.

Backward pass

At the final node, LF equals project duration. LS = LF − duration. If an activity has several successors, its LF is the minimum LS of all immediate successors.

Total Float = LS − ES = LF − EF

UNIT 07 · CPM UPDATE

Review date, remaining work and critical-path shift

Real exam questions often mutate the network after some work has already been completed. The trick is to stop treating the original network as frozen.

At a review date
Completed activities are removed from the remaining problem. Their actual completion time is fixed. Remaining activity durations are replaced by the remaining durations. Then recompute the remaining network and add the elapsed time.

Shadow price
The marginal improvement in objective value for one additional unit of a binding resource, within the allowable RHS range.

For simultaneous RHS changes, use the fraction of each change consumed from its allowable range. If the total ≤ 100%, the same shadow-price logic remains valid (subject to the report conditions).

CHAPTER 1 · DECISION ANALYSIS

Turn a business story into a payoff problem

A decision problem always contains two different kinds of things. The decision maker controls one set of choices; the world controls another set of outcomes. In DAB, the controlled choices are the decision alternatives and the uncontrolled outcomes are the states of nature.

Suppose a retailer can order 1, 2 or 3 lots before demand becomes known. “Order 1 lot” is a decision. “High demand” is not a decision; it is a state of nature. The payoff cell is what the chosen action earns when that state occurs.

Step 1

What can I choose?

List the alternatives. These become rows in a payoff table.

Step 2

What can I not control?

List the states of nature. These become columns.

Step 3

What is the consequence?

Write profit, cost, time or another payoff in every cell.

Exam habit: before calculating, write “Decision = …; State = …; Payoff = …; Probability = …; Criterion = …”. That five-line setup often reveals which method the examiner expects.

CHAPTER 2 · UNCERTAINTY

When probabilities are missing, the criterion changes the decision

“No probabilities” does not mean “no method”. It means that you choose a rule that represents how cautious or optimistic the decision maker is.

Maximax asks “What is the best payoff I can possibly get?” Maximin asks “What is the best worst-case payoff?” Minimax regret asks “Which decision keeps my maximum feeling of having chosen badly as small as possible?” Hurwicz explicitly mixes the best and worst outcomes using an optimism coefficient α. Laplace treats the states as equally likely.

Payoff table

Find each row maximum → choose the largest.

Payoff table

Maximin

Find each row minimum → choose the largest.

Regret table

Minimax regret

Column maximum − cell payoff → row maximum → choose smallest.

The important point is that regret is not a second payoff table. It is a table of opportunity lost relative to the best decision that could have been made after the state became known.

CHAPTER 3 · EMV + INFORMATION

Expected value is a probability-weighted decision, not an average of rows

With probabilities, each decision has an expected monetary value. Multiply every payoff in a row by the probability of its state and add them. You do this row by row because the decision maker chooses the row.

EMV(dᵢ) = Σ [P(Sⱼ) × Vᵢⱼ]

For perfect information, the timing changes. If I knew the state before choosing, I would take the best cell in each column. Therefore EPPI is built column-wise. EVPI is simply the value of knowing the state perfectly compared with the best decision without that knowledge.

EVPI = EPPI − max(EMV without information)

Cost version: when the entries are costs rather than profits, “best” means the minimum cost in each state. The direction of the optimisation changes, but the logic does not.

CHAPTER 4 · DECISION TREES + BAYES

Rollback means solve the future before choosing the present

A decision tree makes chronology visible. Use a square for a decision node and a circle for a chance node. At a chance node, take a probability-weighted expectation. At a decision node, take the best branch. Then move right-to-left until you reach the initial decision.

Bayes questions look intimidating because they introduce conditional probabilities, but the computation is disciplined. For signal E:

P(Sᵢ|E) = P(Sᵢ ∩ E) / P(E) = P(E|Sᵢ)P(Sᵢ) / P(E)

In many exam questions the posterior probabilities are already supplied. Do not “re-Bayes” them unless the question asks you to. Use the posterior probabilities at the information branch, select the best decision inside that branch, then weight the branch values by the signal probabilities.

CHAPTER 5 · CPM

Critical path is the longest chain, and float comes from the gap around an activity

In a precedence network, the forward pass tells you the earliest dates at which activities can happen. For an activity with predecessors, ES = maximum predecessor EF. Then EF = ES + duration.

The backward pass works from the project completion time. For an activity with successors, LF = minimum successor LS. Then LS = LF − duration.

Total Float = LS − ES = LF − EF

An activity with zero total float is critical. The project completion time is the length of the longest start-to-finish path. During a progress review, do not simply carry the original critical path forward. Remove the work already completed, put residual durations on the remaining network, and find the new longest remaining path.

CHAPTER 7 · SENSITIVITY + IP

Shadow price is a marginal value, not a magic multiplier

If a constraint is binding and its shadow price is ₹7, then—within the allowable RHS range—one additional unit of that resource improves the objective by ₹7 for a maximisation problem. The phrase “within the allowable range” is essential.

If RHS rises from 300 to 325 and the shadow price is 7, then the objective change is 25×7. But if the RHS rises beyond the allowable increase, the current basis may change. You must not blindly extend the old shadow price.

Integer programming adds a logical requirement: some variables must be integers, and binary variables are restricted to 0 or 1. Facility opening is a classic example: a binary variable decides whether capacity becomes available; a linking constraint then prevents shipments through a facility that is not opened.

CHAPTER 8 · TRANSPORTATION + ASSIGNMENT

Same optimisation logic, different business stories

Transportation asks how much to send from each source to each destination. The objective is normally to minimise total shipping cost. A balanced problem has total supply = total demand; an unbalanced problem is balanced by adding a dummy source or destination with the appropriate residual quantity.

Assignment is the one-to-one special case: each person gets exactly one job and each job gets exactly one person. Use binary variables xᵢⱼ. A row and a column each sum to one. For small 3×3 examples, explicit enumeration is perfectly exam-friendly and makes the one-to-one logic visible.

CHAPTER 9 · EXAM SCRIPT

What your six-mark answer should physically look like

  1. Given / model setup — variables, states, probabilities, or a small table.
  2. Formula / rule — one clean line before substitution.
  3. Working — each calculation on a separate line.
  4. Comparison — show the alternatives side-by-side where appropriate.
  5. Decision + interpretation — “Therefore choose … because …”.

This format is not extra writing. It is how you make partial credit possible if arithmetic slips and how you demonstrate that you understood the model.
